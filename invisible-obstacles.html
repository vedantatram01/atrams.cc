Invisible Obstacles

One date field stopped every blind screen-reader user we tested from finishing a job application. This is why, and how I redesigned the form to work with a screen reader from start to finish.

The short version

Online forms are one of the most common places the web breaks for people who use screen readers. On a job application the cost is high: a field you can’t complete can end the application before anyone reads it.

5×longer for blind users on the same web task when a site is poorly accessible: 424 seconds against 82.Griffith et al., 2022
51%of the top million home pages have form fields a screen reader can’t name.WebAIM, 2026
706kpeople in the US are blind, among 7.15 million with vision loss glasses can’t correct.CDC, 2024

Where it started

Our team asked a simple question: what problems do blind and low-vision users face when they fill in digital forms with a screen reader? To find out, we watched four blind adults complete CareerOneStop’s Practice Job Application, a 7-page form from the US Department of Labor, on their own devices.

Each session had a short interview, the observed task over Zoom with screen sharing, and a reflection. We coded the transcripts line by line into themes, and used task analysis and the AEIOU framework on our observation notes.

What got in the way

Every participant was an experienced screen-reader user, and every breakdown we saw came from how the form was built. Three themes explained them.

Knowing where you are

Unpredictable navigation left people lost.

Focus jumped between sections. The F and H keys skipped information or landed on unlabelled controls, and some keys advanced the page without warning, so people had to stop, re-scan and rebuild their picture of the form.

Controls that just work

The date field stopped everyone.

No one could tell the required format, operate the date control reliably from the keyboard, or make sense of what the screen reader said about it. People skipped the field or tried workaround after workaround.

Clear feedback

No feedback meant doing the form’s job for it.

The form never said when a required field was missed or an entry was wrong. Participants re-checked every answer by hand and tracked their own progress in their heads.

Designing the date field

The date field was the most severe barrier. My first question was whether it needed to exist at all. The original’s Date field asks for today’s date, which the system already knows, so the redesign records it automatically when you submit and shows it on the review page.

Applicants still have to enter dates only they know, like when they could start. The original uses the browser’s own date input for these, so “use native controls” wasn’t enough on its own. I compared four ways to ask for a date.

The redesign, step by step

Each part of the redesign responds to one of the three findings. The numbered notes come from tags in the prototype’s code; hover or focus a note to highlight it on the screen.

Design decisions

Every decision starts from something we saw or heard in the four sessions. Where the sessions didn’t settle a question, I used published research or accessibility standards. Anything that is my own call is labelled that way. Open a decision to see why.

  • Today’s date is recorded automatically, not typedMy call

    The original asks for a date the system already knows, and that field stopped every participant. Removing it takes the hardest barrier off the first page for everyone.

    Basis: The application date is captured on submit and shown on the review page

  • Nothing is asked twiceMy call

    The original asks if you’re employed now, then asks again whether you still work at your latest job. It also asks for hours a week, then again for full-time or part-time. Each repeat is more listening and another chance for answers to disagree.

    Basis: “Employed now” fills in “I currently work here”; the schedule page only asks when you can’t work

  • Month, Day and Year as three plain text boxesObserved

    None of the four participants could fill in the browser’s built-in date box or work out what format it wanted.

    Basis: All 4 participants: none could enter a date in the built-in box or tell what format it wanted

  • Standard radio buttons, checkboxes and dropdownsObserved

    These worked with every participant’s screen reader. The built-in date box was the one control that failed.

    Basis: Participants got through these controls without help; the date box was the only one that failed

  • A heading for every step and section; every field has a nameObserved + WCAG

    Participants moved by jumping from heading to heading and field to field. Missing headings and unnamed fields made them skip content or land on a box with no name.

    Basis: Participants jumped by heading (H key) and by field (F key). WCAG 1.3.1 Info and Relationships, 2.4.6 Headings and Labels

  • No pop-ups, auto-advance or surprise redirectsOur recommendation + WCAG

    Our study’s recommendations name these as a cause of the screen reader’s position jumping without warning.

    Basis: Written by our team after the sessions. WCAG 3.2.2 On Input: no change of page or focus without warning

  • A list of errors at the top that takes focus, plus a message next to each fieldResearch + WCAG

    The original never said what was wrong. A published study with blind users found that an error list like this reduces input mistakes.

    Basis: Buzzi et al., 2016: an error summary cut input mistakes for blind users. WCAG 3.3.1 Error Identification

  • Follow-up questions appear only when they applyMy call

    The original shows every “If yes…” question to everyone, so screen-reader users hear questions that don’t apply to them.

    Basis: Participants had to listen to “If yes…” questions that didn’t apply to them

  • Focus moves to the new step’s heading; the step number is in the page titleMy call + WCAG

    Participants kept track of their progress in their heads. This tells them where they’ve landed and how far along they are.

    Basis: Participants tracked their step in their heads. WCAG 2.4.2 Page Titled, 2.4.3 Focus Order

  • A review page with a Change link for each sectionObserved + WCAG

    Participants went back and re-checked every answer by hand. The original has no review page.

    Basis: Participants re-read every answer by hand. WCAG 3.3.4 Error Prevention: let people review before submitting

  • “(optional)” written in words, never an asteriskWCAG

    Screen readers read words exactly. An asterisk may be skipped or read out as “star”.

    Basis: 3.3.2 Labels or Instructions. An asterisk may be skipped or read as “star”

  • When an employer is added, focus moves to it and it’s announcedWCAG

    The page never changes without the screen reader saying so.

    Basis: 4.1.3 Status Messages, 2.4.3 Focus Order

  • Animation only shows what changed; it never moves focusWCAG

    Turns off when the device is set to reduce motion, so it never gets in the way.

    Basis: 2.3.3 Animation from Interactions; follows the device’s reduce-motion setting

Validation

An automated scan with axe-core against WCAG 2.1 A and AA found no violations on any of the 8 steps, including every error state. A scripted run of a complete application exercised every validation rule, the follow-up questions, adding and removing employers, and the review.

Automated checks only cover what code can detect. The next step is to test the redesign with VoiceOver and NVDA, then with blind screen-reader users on the same task as the original study, measuring independent completion, time on task and confidence.

Limitations

  • Four participants, all in Chicago and all experienced screen-reader users, so the findings may not hold for newer users.
  • One type of form, observed remotely over Zoom.
  • The redesign has passed automated checks but hasn’t yet been tested with blind users.
