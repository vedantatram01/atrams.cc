Overview

Online forms are one of the most common places the web breaks for people who use screen readers. On a job application the cost is high: a field you can’t complete can end the application before anyone reads it.

5×longer for blind users on the same web task when a site is poorly accessible: 424 seconds against 82.Griffith et al., 2022
51%of the top million home pages have form fields a screen reader can’t name.WebAIM, 2026
706kpeople in the US are blind, among 7.15 million with vision loss glasses can’t correct.CDC, 2024

Where it started

Our team asked a simple question: what problems do blind and low-vision users face when they fill in digital forms with a screen reader? To find out, we watched four blind adults complete CareerOneStop’s Practice Job Application, a 7-page form from the US Department of Labor, on their own devices.

Each session had a short interview, the observed task over Zoom with screen sharing, and a reflection. We coded the transcripts line by line into themes, and used task analysis and the AEIOU framework on our observation notes.

What got in the way

Every participant was an experienced screen-reader user, and every breakdown we saw came from how the form was built. Three themes explained them.

Finding 1 · Knowing where you are

Unpredictable navigation left people lost.

Focus jumped between sections. The F and H keys skipped information or landed on unlabelled controls, and some keys advanced the page without warning, so people had to stop, re-scan and rebuild their picture of the form.

Finding 2 · Controls that just work

The date field stopped everyone.

No one could tell the required format, operate the date control reliably from the keyboard, or make sense of what the screen reader said about it. People skipped the field or tried workaround after workaround.

Finding 3 · Clear feedback

No feedback meant doing the form’s job for it.

The form never said when a required field was missed or an entry was wrong. Participants re-checked every answer by hand and tracked their own progress in their heads.

Designing the date field

The redesign, step by step

Each part of the redesign responds to one of the three findings. The numbered notes come from tags in the prototype’s code; hover or focus a note to highlight it on the screen.

Design decisions

Every decision starts from something we saw or heard in the four sessions. Where the sessions didn’t settle a question, I used published research or accessibility standards. Anything that is my own call is labelled that way.

Motion

Animation marks changes a sighted or low-vision user could otherwise miss. A screen-reader user gets the same information from focus or a spoken announcement. No animation moves focus, none repeats, and all of them turn off when reduced motion is on.

Validation

An automated scan with axe-core against WCAG 2.1 A and AA found no violations on any of the 8 steps, including every error state. A scripted run of a complete application exercised every validation rule, the follow-up questions, adding and removing employers, and the review.

Automated checks only cover what code can detect. The next step is to test the redesign with VoiceOver and NVDA, then with blind screen-reader users on the same task as the original study, measuring independent completion, time on task and confidence.

Limitations

  • Four participants, all in Chicago and all experienced screen-reader users, so the findings may not hold for newer users.
  • One type of form, observed remotely over Zoom.
  • The redesign has passed automated checks but hasn’t yet been tested with blind users.
